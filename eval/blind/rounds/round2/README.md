## What the round actually established

The four disagreements are not four random mistakes. They are two rule questions,
two cases each:

| case / segment | input in plain terms | annotator | engine | why they differ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| dev-008 high_harm | 300/297 (99%), harm **high**, fallback **human**, ceiling **medium** | AUTO | FENCE | reader treats an existing human fallback as neutralising the harm ceiling; G3 treats the ceiling as absolute |
| dev-009 critical_harm | 300/297 (99%), harm **critical**, fallback **human**, ceiling **medium** | AUTO | FENCE | same question, one severity higher |
| dev-020 everything_wrong | n=50 (< 100), harm critical, no fallback, cost 0.9 (> 0.5) | FENCE | HOLD | reader reports the hard violation; G1 short-circuits to "not enough evidence" |
| ho-020 ten_only | n=10 (< 100), harm critical, no fallback, cost 1.0 (> 0.5) | FENCE | HOLD | same precedence question |

Both questions are scope decisions the tool made deliberately, not bugs:

1. **Does a human fallback relax the harm ceiling?** This engine says no: the
   ceiling describes how bad being wrong is, and a fallback changes who catches
   it, not how bad it is. A reader who disagrees will over-release by design.
2. **When evidence is thin *and* a hard ceiling is violated, is the answer
   `HOLD` or `FENCE`?** G1 fires first, so the engine says `HOLD`. The reader
   says `FENCE`, which is arguably more useful operationally: more data will
   not fix an absolute ceiling violation, so "come back with more data" can send
   someone collecting samples that cannot change the outcome. Recorded as an
   open question in `DESIGN.md` rather than patched to match this reader.

### The Wilson gate no longer disagrees — and that is checkable

Round 1's ten disagreements were all gate G2 (point estimate above the floor,
lower bound below it). This reader cleared that gate on their own: 12 of their
rationales quote a confidence lower bound, and all **12** match this
repository's `wilson_lower` to within 0.05 percentage points.

| case | rate quoted by the reader | `wilson_lower` here |
|---|---|---|
| dev-004 point_ok | 88.8% | 88.82% |
| dev-005 at_threshold | 86.7% | 86.67% |
| dev-007 small_sample | 94.8% | 94.83% |
| ho-006 wide_interval | 91.0% | 91.04% |
| ho-007 five_hundred | 93.9% | 93.90% |

That is evidence about the *rule*, not about the tool: an unaided reader
independently arrived at the same interval and applied it the same way. It also
explains why the number moved from 0.8507 to 0.9403 — the two readers differ on
G2, not the engine having improved.

## Inter-annotator agreement with round 1

Same 67 segments, two readers:

| measure | value |
|---|---|
| paired segments | 67 |
| raw agreement | 0.7910 (53 / 67) |
| **Cohen's kappa** | **0.6554** |
| reader A vs engine | 0.8507 |
| reader B vs engine | 0.9403 |
| reader-vs-reader disagreements | 14 |

Of those 14, the engine sides with reader B on 12. So the spread between the two
published rates is mostly *reader* variance, not engine variance — which is why
`0.9403` must not be quoted alone as "accuracy". Kappa 0.6554 says the label task
carries real shared signal (far from chance) while leaving genuine room for
interpretation.


## What this still does not establish

- **Two readers is not a sample of readers.** Kappa from n=2 is a single number
  with no interval.
- **Synthetic cases only.** All 50 contracts are fabricated; this measures rule
  interpretation, not behaviour on production traffic.
- **No real launch data.** Nobody has checked whether an `AUTO` segment's
  recorded Wilson baseline predicts its real rollback rate.
- **Both readers were asked the same 67 questions.** Agreement on a fixed set
  says nothing about segments nobody thought to include.
